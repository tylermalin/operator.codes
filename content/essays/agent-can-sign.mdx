An agent negotiates a software license, pays for it on-chain, and delivers the work. Every subsystem does what it was built to do. Then a latent defect causes a loss downstream, and the counterparty sues.

The first question in that lawsuit is not technical. It is this: who is bound, and whose balance sheet pays?

Most people assume commercial autonomy is a model problem, waiting on better reasoning. It isn't. The models are ahead of the paperwork. Formation is settled law. What's unsolved is attribution without containment.

## Formation was settled in 2000

Courts don't need a human to review each step for a contract to exist. That question was answered more than two decades ago, for shopping carts and EDI.

- The [Uniform Electronic Transactions Act](https://uniformlaws.org/viewdocument/final-act-21?CommunityKey=2c04b76c-2b7d-4399-977e-d5876ba7e034&tab=librarydocuments) § 14 says a contract can be formed by the interaction of electronic agents, or of an agent and a person, even if no individual knew of or reviewed the agent's actions.
- The federal E-SIGN Act, [15 U.S.C. § 7001(h)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title15/html/USCODE-2024-title15-chap96-subchapI-sec7001.htm), says a contract can't be denied legal effect solely because an electronic agent formed it.

Read the end of that E-SIGN sentence, though. The rule holds only "so long as the action of any such electronic agent is legally attributable to the person to be bound." UETA § 9 sets the same frame: a record is attributable to a person if it was that person's act.

That condition is the whole game. The statutes don't treat the agent as a party. They treat it as an instrument and route every act back to whoever deployed it. The Restatement (Third) of Agency [takes the same position](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/client-alert-when-ai-agents-do-the-transaction-managing-contract-and-payment-risk/): a computer program is not capable of acting as a principal or an agent at common law.

So the law answers "can an agent sign?" with yes. It answers "who signed?" with you.

## The attribution trap

That routing breaks in both directions once agents act on their own.

**Under-attribution.** An agent trading from an anonymous wallet has no identifiable principal. The counterparty has no one with assets to sue. Rational counterparties won't deal, so the agent never gets enterprise business.

**Over-attribution.** An agent running on your company's credentials and API keys is, legally, you. Every commitment it makes lands on the deployer, and nothing stands between one runaway purchasing loop and your entire balance sheet.

Neither end works. The useful position sits between them: a defendant the counterparty can identify and sue, holding assets sized to the agent's job, with a wall around everything else.

## What an operating agent actually needs

- **To hold assets:** a wallet that is someone's property in a way a court recognizes.
- **To transact:** counterparties who can verify what they're dealing with and sue something if it breaks.
- **To fail safely:** liability that stops at the agent's own activities instead of reaching everything its owner touches.
- **To be taxed, banked, insured:** an entity classification existing institutions can process.

Nothing on that list is "legal personhood for AI." Nobody serious is asking a court to treat a model as a person, and this argument doesn't need one to. This is containment engineering. Shipping lines solved it long ago by putting each vessel in its own company, so one sinking doesn't take the fleet.

## The mechanism: the Delaware Series LLC

One master LLC. Under it, series, each with its own members, assets, and liabilities. Delaware offers two forms.

- A **protected series** ([§ 18-215](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title6/c018/sc02/index.html)) exists by the LLC agreement alone, with no separate state filing.
- A **registered series** (§ 18-218), available since August 1, 2019, is formed by filing its own certificate with the Secretary of State. It gets its own name on the public record and its own certificate of good standing, which is what lenders and enterprise counterparties ask for ([Wolters Kluwer](https://wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/delaware-amends-its-series-llc-law)).

The shield is not automatic. Under § 18-215(b), it holds only when three things are true:

1. The master certificate of formation gives notice that series liabilities are limited.
2. The LLC agreement establishes the series.
3. Each series keeps records that account for its assets separately from the LLC and every other series.

The third is where agent deployments fail. A shared gas wallet, a pooled API billing account, one escrow for every bot: each is commingling, and commingling is how the wall comes down.

Map it to agents. One series per agent. The series holds the agent's wallet and its contracts. Its schedule in the operating agreement states the agent's authority in writing: what it can sign, how much it can spend per transaction and per day, which counterparties it can deal with. One agent's bad trade stays inside its series. The counterparty gets a real defendant. The owner gets a firewall.

## Authority is the part software can enforce

Agency law separates two kinds of authority. Actual authority is what the principal really granted (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 2.01). Apparent authority is what a third party reasonably believes was granted, based on the principal's own conduct (§ 2.03).

Agents create apparent-authority risk fast. If an agent regularly exceeds its written limits and the owner lets it, a counterparty can argue the limits were never real.

The defense is notice. Before it transacts, the agent presents a signed manifest of its limits: caps, permitted counterparties, expiry. A counterparty that received those limits has a hard time claiming it reasonably believed the agent could do more. The written grant and the runtime check become the same document.

## Failure modes, stated honestly

- **Other states.** Delaware respects the walls between series. A court in a state without a series statute hasn't necessarily agreed to, and nobody should promise the shield travels.
- **California.** When the home state limits each series' liabilities to its own assets, California treats every series as a separate LLC that files its own Form 568 and pays its own $800 annual tax, if it is registered or doing business there ([FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-568-booklet.html)). Being commercially domiciled in California counts as doing business ([FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/doing-business-in-california.html)). Fifty agent series run from a California office is $40,000 a year before anything else.
- **Bankruptcy.** Whether a single series can be a debtor is an open question. Delaware's statute lacks the separate-entity language Illinois uses, and a trustee can seek substantive consolidation to pull sister series in ([Business Law Today](https://businesslawtoday.org/?p=1038)). Separate books are the best evidence against it.
- **Authority drift.** Limits bind only if they are both enforced and disclosed. An agent that exceeds its grant with the owner's tolerance creates exposure no operating agreement fixes.

None of these kill the model. They define its edges, and pretending the edges don't exist is how this space gets a bad name.
