An agent negotiates a price, executes the transaction, delivers the work. Every step functioned. Then something goes wrong, and the first legal question has no answer: who is bound?

The instinct is that this is a technology problem waiting on better AI. It isn't. The models are ahead of the paperwork.

## The attribution trap

Yes, contracts formed by automated systems are already enforceable. UETA and E-SIGN settled that two decades ago for shopping carts and EDI.

But those statutes work by attribution: the contract binds the person who deployed the system. The agent is treated as a tool, like a fax machine with opinions.

That attribution model breaks in both directions for autonomous agents:

- **Under-attribution.** An agent with no identifiable principal binds no one, so counterparties have no recourse and rational ones won't deal.
- **Over-attribution.** An agent attributed to you personally binds you completely, with no liability boundary between the agent's decisions and your entire balance sheet.

The real issue: formation was never the problem. Attribution without containment is.

## What an operating agent actually needs

- **To hold assets:** a wallet that is someone's property in a way a court recognizes.
- **To transact:** counterparties who can verify what they're dealing with and sue something if it breaks.
- **To fail safely:** liability that stops at the agent's own activities instead of reaching everything its owner touches.
- **To be taxed, banked, insured:** an entity classification that existing institutions can process.

Nothing in that list is "legal personhood for AI." Nobody serious is asking a court to treat a model as a person, and this argument doesn't need them to. This is standard containment engineering, the same reason shipping companies put each vessel in its own subsidiary.

## The mechanism: the Delaware Series LLC

One master LLC filing. Under it, individual series, each with its own assets, liabilities, and internal governance, walled off from the others by statute (Delaware LLC Act §18-215).

Why this shape fits agents specifically: series are cheap and fast to create (no separate state filing per protected series), so one-agent-one-series scales the way agents actually get deployed, in numbers.

The wrapper logic: a human-owned series holds the agent's wallet and contracts. The operating agreement encodes the agent's authority in writing: what it can sign, spending limits, counterparty classes. The agent acts as an authorized agent of the series in the ordinary legal sense of the word.

One agent's tort or bad trade is contained to that series' assets. The counterparty gets a real defendant. The owner gets a firewall.

## Failure modes, stated honestly

- Series liability shields are Delaware-tested, not everywhere-tested. Some states' courts haven't ruled on respecting internal series walls, and California taxes each series as a separate entity anyway.
- Bankruptcy treatment of an individual series is thin case law. Anyone claiming certainty here is selling something.
- Authority drift: an agent acting beyond its encoded scope creates apparent-authority exposure. The operating agreement limits are only as good as the counterparty's notice of them.

None of these kill the model. They define its current edges, and pretending the edges don't exist is how this space gets a bad name.
