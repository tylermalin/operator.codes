## The one thing to understand

Venture is back. It is not back for you by default.

Q1 2026 recorded $30.4 billion in startup funding, ahead of last year. Down rounds are at 2019 levels. Dilution is down. Terms favor founders. Every headline number is green.

Underneath, the seed market is doing something specific: more dollars, fewer companies. Seed capital rose 25% year over year while seed deal count fell 21%. The companies getting a first check are raising more than ever. The companies not getting one are not getting a smaller check. They are getting nothing, or a bridge.

That is the operating environment. The rest of this guide is about how to be on the right side of it.

## Part 1: Which market are you actually in?

The most useful thing in the Carta data is that it stopped reporting one seed market and started reporting three. They are priced differently enough that blended benchmarks will mislead you.

**Series A median post-money valuation, Q1 2026**

| Category | Post-money | Round size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-AI | $55M | $7.0M |
| AI applied | $65M | $8.9M |
| AI foundational model | $300M | $40.0M |

**Seed medians, Q1 2026**

| Category | Post-money | Round size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-AI | $20.6M | $2.0M |
| AI applied | $21.5M | $2.9M |
| AI foundational model | $250M | $9.0M |

Three things to take from these tables.

First, at seed the gap between non-AI and applied AI is small. About a million dollars of post-money and a million of round size. "AI" on the deck is not buying you a materially different price at seed.

Second, the foundational model column is not a benchmark. It is a different asset class. A few dozen companies training models are raising at valuations that would have been Series C outcomes three years ago. If you are not training models, ignore that column entirely. It will only distort your expectations.

Third, and this is the part most founders miss: the applied-AI premium is shrinking at later stages. At Series C, applied AI priced 1.86x over non-AI in Q1 2025. In Q1 2026 it priced 1.24x. The market is learning that "built on a model" is not a moat. Expect that repricing to keep working its way down toward seed.

**Decision rule:** Benchmark against the row you belong to. Not the row you aspire to.

## Part 2: What a seed round looks like right now

**Valuation.** Median seed post-money is $21.6 million. The interquartile range is $12.4M to $35M. That is a tight band, which means seed is the one stage where the median is a usable anchor. If you are pitching above $35M without foundational-model characteristics or a hardware moat, you are in the top quartile and should have a specific reason.

**The 90th percentile.** $78.8 million at seed, nearly double where it stood two years ago. The gap between the median seed round and the top decile is the widest it has ever been. This matters because the deals you hear about are top-decile deals. They are not your comps.

**Dilution.** Median seed dilution is 19% in the Carta dataset overall, 13% for non-AI companies and 15% for AI companies in the last two quarters. AI founders give up slightly more equity in exchange for a higher post. That is a fair trade at seed if the higher post is real.

**Terms.** Liquidation preferences above 1x appeared in 2.7% of primary rounds. Participation rights in 5.4%. Multipliers in 3.3%. All at or near multi-year lows. Any of these on a seed primary is an outlier, and you should treat it as one in negotiation.

**Time to Series A.** Median is now an even two years, and it is starting to compress. Two years ago it was closer to 2.2. The front of the funnel is speeding up for the companies that get through it.

**Age at raise.** The median company raising a Series A is 2.8 years old. Plan your seed to fund roughly 24 months of operating, with the A closing before month 30.

## Part 3: The non-AI tax is real

Here is the number that should change how you plan if you are not an AI company.

Seed pre-money valuations fell 6.6% year over year. Series A fell 7.1%. Bridge valuations fell harder: down 18% at seed, 16% at Series A. Meanwhile Series B and C valuations rose 17.2% and 12.5%.

That 6.6% covers all seed rounds, not just non-AI ones. But Carta's read is that the pressure falls on non-AI: investors are pricing non-AI early-stage deals more conservatively because the AI premium is absorbing their attention and their reserves. There is a second reading, and the report names it in the conclusion: this may be the first legible signal that investors are beginning to price in what capable AI replaces.

The market is starting to ask a specific question at seed: why does a capable model not do this? If your answer is "we will move faster than incumbents" or "we have a better UI," you will feel the 6.6%.

The categories that are not feeling it share one trait. Hardware leads all industries in median post-money at $90 million (seed through Series B). Crypto/Web is second at $80.8 million. Fintech at $58.6 million, energy at $52.1 million. SaaS sits at $46.5 million, below the midpoint. The premium categories are harder to replicate. The moat is structural: capital intensity, regulatory position, physical infrastructure, network effects that require real-world deployment. Not a product decision.

**Decision rule:** Your defensibility slide must answer the AI-replacement question directly. If it cannot, price at the non-AI median or below and plan for a longer raise.

## Part 4: The hardware playbook (and why software founders should study it)

Hardware is the quiet winner in this dataset. Carta calls out a specific mechanism: non-dilutive debt is letting hardware founders build before they raise. They arrive at institutional rounds with product, revenue, and a cleaner cap table than prior generations, and the valuations reflect it.

That playbook generalizes. The core move is this: use capital that does not take equity to reach the milestone that changes your valuation tier, then raise equity from a stronger position.

For hardware that means equipment financing, revenue-based financing, and grants. For software the analogs are customer prepayment, design-partner contracts that fund development, and grant capital where it exists (climate, defense, health). The point is not the instrument. The point is arriving at the priced round with proof rather than a plan.

Austin is worth noting here. 64.8% of Austin's capital over the last two quarters went to hardware. Its seed median ($22.6M) is within 20% of the Bay Area ($27.5M). If you are raising hardware and your investor list is entirely coastal, you are missing the second-largest concentration of relevant capital in the country.

## Part 5: Bridges, and when to take one

Bridge rounds now make up more rounds than capital at every stage, per Carta. Measured by dollars, 16.6% of venture capital raised on Carta in Q2 2025 came through bridges, and 22.5% among Series A companies ([Hustle Fund, citing Carta](https://www.hustlefund.vc/post/angel-squad-bridge-rounds-the-fundraising-move-nobody-talks-about-but-everyone-needs)). They are normal. Most companies raising in this market will take one at some point.

They are not free. Bridge valuations at seed are down 18% from a year ago, and Carta reports bridges carry more investor-protective terms than primaries, where participation rights appear in only 5.4% of rounds and preferences above 1x in 2.7%.

Carta's framing is the right one: a bridge is not a strategic financing choice. It is a continuation of capital while the company earns its way to the next primary round.

**Decision rule:** Take a bridge only if it funds a specific, named milestone that moves you into a different valuation tier at the next primary. A bridge taken to "wait for better conditions" is a slow down round with worse terms.

## Part 6: If you have a down round in your history

The stigma is gone. 96.1% of Q1 capital went to up rounds. The down-round rate is 11.4%, back to pre-bull-market levels. Investors have absorbed enough reset stories that a clean narrative is sufficient: what was reset, what changed, what you built since.

Lead with the arc. Do not bury it in an appendix. A founder who explains the 2023 reset in the first five minutes reads as operationally honest. A founder who lets the investor find it in diligence reads as something else.

## Part 7: Where the money is

Geography still matters more than the remote-work narrative suggests.

California alone took 48% of all U.S. venture capital on Carta over the last four quarters. The West region took 59.9% in Q1. West plus Northeast is roughly 80 cents of every dollar.

Bay Area seed median is $27.5M. Austin $22.6M. Boston $20M. Los Angeles $23.3M. New York $23.2M. The Bay Area premium is real at seed (about 20% over the next tier) but narrows by Series B, where Seattle tied the Bay Area at $200M.

AI concentration by metro: Bay Area 70.9%, Austin 60%, DC 53.6%, Seattle 52.1%, Boston 48.1%, Los Angeles 38.8%. LA has the lowest AI share and the most diversified industry mix, which is either resilience or a ceiling depending on what you are building.

**Decision rule:** Raise where your category concentrates, not where you live. Hardware in Austin. Health in Boston. Foundational AI in the Bay Area. Everything else, the Bay Area is still the deepest pool but not the only one.

## Part 8: Liquidity is a decade away. Plan for it.

The median company raising a Series E+ round is 9.5 years old. Only half of 2025 venture-backed tech IPOs priced above their last private valuation. Less than a third stayed above their IPO price after listing. The IPO window is cracking open (34 IPOs in Q1, $9.9B raised, Cerebras 20x oversubscribed), but narrowly, for AI infrastructure and fintech with clean unit economics.

For a seed-stage company this has three practical consequences.

**Employee equity.** Your option plan needs to assume a ten-year horizon. Refresh grants, extended exercise windows, and tender access are retention tools, not perks. Design them now, when the cap table is simple.

**Secondaries and tenders.** Nearly 100 tender offers worth $10.1 billion were visible to Carta in the first five months of 2026, already ahead of all of 2025. The tender is now the standard liquidity mechanism, with four distinct uses: permanent IPO alternative, retention weapon, pre-IPO sequencing, and cap table cleanup. That fourth use matters at seed. Angels approaching fund end, early advisors with small stakes, and departed employees accumulate on the cap table. A structured tender before a priced round is a governance tool.

**Investor selection.** Ask every seed investor how their fund handles a ten-year hold. Fund life, reserve policy, and secondary appetite are diligence questions you should be asking them.

## Part 9: What the winning decks actually did

The Carta data tells you where the market is priced. It does not tell you what the companies clearing the gate showed investors. For that, a sample of 37 seed and pre-seed decks that closed in 2025 is useful, with six of them broken down slide by slide (Wonder, Alice.tech, Digs, Hypernatural, RMFG, Reducto).

Before drawing lessons, read the sample for what it is.

These are top-decile outcomes, not comps. The median round in the gallery is $7 million. Carta's median seed round is $2.0M for non-AI and $2.9M for applied AI. The 75th percentile is $4.8M and $5.5M. The median deck in this gallery raised more than three quarters of all seed rounds. The smallest raise in the sample ($2.5M, pre-seed) is roughly the Carta seed median. Roughly 70% of the decks are AI companies, which tracks Carta's 60%+ share of capital. Every pattern below is a pattern among winners at the top of the distribution. Copying the format does not buy you the outcome; the underlying substance did.

With that caveat, six patterns repeat across the decks that closed.

1. **Thirteen slides.** Wonder, Alice.tech, Hypernatural, and Reducto all ran 13 slides. The full sample clusters between 9 and 14. The outliers (27, 34 slides) exist but are rare. Rule: if you cannot make the case in 13, the problem is the case, not the slide count.
2. **Team early.** Alice.tech and Hypernatural put the team on slide 3. Wonder on slide 5. In a market where investors are concentrating capital into fewer companies, founder pedigree is being read as risk reduction before the product is even shown. Repeat founders, prior exits, and recognizable operating backgrounds appear in every one of the six. RMFG's solo founder taught himself to code while working gas station maintenance, and still led with the YC credential and a Collison angel check. If you have the pedigree, put it early. If you do not, put proof of execution early instead (RMFG: 100k parts shipped by a two-person engineering team).
3. **A one-line analogy for positioning.** "Duolingo for exams." "Midjourney or Canva for video." "Next-generation contract manufacturer." Each gives the investor a mental model in under five seconds and implies the business model, the scale, and the category. This matters more in 2026 than it did before because the applied-AI premium is compressing: "AI for X" no longer carries information. The analogy has to name what is different about the business, not what model it sits on.
4. **Reframe out of the crowded category.** Reducto did not pitch an AI model. It pitched data ingestion, the unglamorous layer models depend on, and showed real ChatGPT and Claude failures on real Ameriprise documents to make the problem visceral. RMFG did not pitch a machine shop. It pitched software-defined manufacturing inside the reshoring wave. This is the deck-level version of the Carta replacement question: the winning decks answer "why does a capable model not do this" by moving to a layer the model cannot reach.
5. **Stack the problem before showing the product.** RMFG spent three consecutive slides building the problem (capacity, vertical integration cost, 10x worse for custom assemblies) before revealing the solution. Reducto spent three showing failures. The effect is that the product reads as inevitable rather than clever. Decks that lead with the product and back into the problem are the ones that end up in the "another AI tool" pile.
6. **Traction shaped to what you have.** Nobody in the sample had clean SaaS metrics at seed, and none of them pretended to.

- Alice.tech had revenue and showed the curve: 2x MRR in six weeks, 15% week over week, plus cohort engagement data. Exact figures redacted.
- Reducto had ten weeks of revenue and showed the shape of the curve with the ARR figure blacked out, then let a customer quote (3x faster, 90% less engineering time) carry the specifics.
- RMFG had unit economics: 42% repeat rate, welding at 29% of revenue on 17 orders, 100% of welding customers upgraded from instant quote. That is a margin expansion story on a pre-seed deck.
- Hypernatural had a growth chart and four full testimonial slides with hard numbers ("hundreds of millions of views").
- Wonder and Digs, with thinner metrics, used partnerships (Google DeepMind, BBC, Universal), patents (11+), scale proxies (10k homes, 50 states), and a named endorsement (Mike Rowe) as the traction substitute.

The rule: pick the strongest form of evidence you have and give it the slide count. Do not force a revenue chart if the honest number is small. Do not hide behind logos if you have a curve.

Two structural notes. Alice.tech's fundraising slide named the Series A milestones (timing, revenue target) and the fund allocation. In a market where the median seed-to-A gap is two years and investors are pricing the next round at the current one, showing the A plan is a signal of discipline, not presumption. And the three non-AI decks in the six (Digs, RMFG, Wonder) all leaned on structural assets: patents, physical output, exclusive partnerships, prior exits. That is the hardware and moat premium from Part 3 showing up at the deck level. Non-AI companies that closed large seeds did it by proving they were hard to replicate.

## Part 10: Your sector has its own rules now

The single most important structural fact in this market: there is no longer one seed bar. There are five, one per sector, and they reward different things. A second sample, 25 companies that closed seed or Series A rounds in 2025 and 2026 across AI/SaaS, ClimateTech, Fintech, HealthTech, and Consumer, makes the divergence concrete. Find your row before you build anything.

**AI / SaaS: prove you are not a wrapper.** This is the defining objection, and every funded deck answered it directly. ManaMind (pre-seed, $1.5M) built a 17-billion-parameter proprietary vision model rather than calling a general LLM. Artisan AI ($25M Series A) sold autonomous agents that run in the background, not assistants that need prompting. Certifyde ($2M) owned the "last mile" adoption problem (88% of companies buy AI, most never scale it) instead of competing on model quality. The pattern: the moat is proprietary tech, autonomy, or a unique data asset, and the TAM-SAM-SOM slide is gone, replaced by a bottom-up monetization wedge. If your defensibility is "we prompt GPT well," you do not clear this bar. This is the deck-level version of the Carta finding that the applied-AI premium is compressing: the market has learned to tell wrappers from moats and is pricing them apart.

**ClimateTech: cut cost, not just carbon, and stack non-dilutive capital.** Every funded climate deck paired venture dollars with a near-term unit-economic advantage over the carbon-intensive incumbent. Voltic's electric cargo ships are pitched as 3 to 4x more profitable than diesel, and it held $100M+ in LOIs before the seed. Dexmat showed a 96% cost reduction and 20x capacity scale since pre-seed. The second move is grants: DOE, DoD, and state matching funds appear in almost every climate raise (Airloom paired a $13.75M round with a $1.25M DoD contract and $5M in Wyoming matching funds). This is the hardware playbook from Part 4 in its purest form. Non-dilutive capital reaches the milestone; equity prices off the proof. If you are in climate and not running a grant strategy in parallel with the raise, you are leaving both money and valuation on the table.

**Fintech: sell structural defensibility, and show profitability.** The zero-interest-rate playbook (growth at any burn) is dead here. Winning fintech decks led with a moat a competitor cannot copy quickly: a bank charter (Mercury's OCC conditional approval), principal Visa membership (Rain), CFTC regulation (Kalshi). Mercury also led with four consecutive years of profitability and a $650M revenue run rate. The lesson for a seed fintech: infrastructure ownership and regulatory position are worth more than growth rate, and unit economics need to be in the deck, not deferred.

**HealthTech: no regulatory or reimbursement wedge, no term sheet.** This is the hardest gate in the sample and the least negotiable. HealthTech led AI megadeals in H1 2025 (9 of 11 raises above $100M), but every funded deck tied its narrative to a specific FDA pathway, a reimbursement mechanism, or deep workflow integration. Mati Health led with an FDA 510(k) in progress and PACS integration. Mindable's entire pitch was a regulatory breakthrough: an FDA change-control plan that lets its AI update without a new clearance each time. Arintra quantified the ROI precisely ($4 to 6M saved per hospital per year, prior-auth cut from 40 minutes to 5). If you are building in health and cannot name your regulatory path and your reimbursement story on one slide each, you are not ready to raise.

**Consumer: the bar is brutal, and profitability is the price of entry.** D2C venture funding fell roughly 97% from its 2021 peak. The consumer companies that closed rounds were almost all either AI hybrids or already near profitability. Poko ($12M Series A) had 3M+ MAUs, 70% organic acquisition, and mid-seven-figure revenue from nine employees. Go DESi raised on a profitable ₹50Cr+ revenue run rate. Antinorm's round was oversubscribed on 2x growth with minimal burn. The tell across the sample: capital efficiency and unit economics are the story, not user growth alone. Mirah's deck did what the AI/SaaS decks did and skipped the TAM slide entirely, pitching a bottom-up wedge instead ($15/month, 100k users target, net dollar retention above 100%). If you are raising consumer without a path to profitability visible in the numbers, assume you are in the 97% that does not close.

The through-line across all five sectors is the same as the Carta data's: the market is concentrating, and it is pricing structural defensibility over growth. What "structural" means differs by sector (proprietary model, cost advantage plus grants, regulatory position, FDA wedge, profitability). Name yours precisely, or the round does not close.

## The larger point

The 2022 reset is over. What replaced it is not the 2021 market. It is a market that has learned to concentrate.

The concentration is the signal. Fewer companies get funded, and the ones that do get funded at sizes and valuations that would have been unusual three years ago. That means the seed round is no longer the cheap option. It is the gate. Clearing it well determines whether the next four years of compression work for you or against you.

The founders who will do well in this market are the ones who understand which of the three markets they are in, price accordingly, and build the moat the market is now pricing: something a capable model cannot replicate on its own.
